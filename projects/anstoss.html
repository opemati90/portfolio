Anstoss: one app for the whole club.

Amateur clubs run on tools never built for them: WhatsApp for chat, Doodle for RSVPs, a PDF for the lineup, a spreadsheet for the table. Anstoss replaces all of it with one white-label app in the club's own colours. A side project I took end to end: research, UX, and code, for Android and iOS.

Research

I started inside the problem

I help run an amateur side, so the tool-chaos was lived, not researched from a distance. To turn that into a brief I mapped every tool a club touches across a match week, ran informal interviews with coaches and admins at other clubs, and read the support threads where volunteers ask each other how to keep a squad organised. Three patterns came back every time.

The coach is also the admin

One volunteer owns comms, attendance, lineup, and the kit. Any tool that adds work, however small, gets dropped by the next fixture.

Information is scattered and stale

The lineup lives in a screenshot, RSVPs in a poll, the table in a browser tab. Nothing is a source of truth, so the same questions get re-asked in the group every week.

The badge does the work

People show up for the crest, not the software. Any club tool that looks generic, or like someone else's brand, never feels like theirs.

Define

What I committed to build

Give a club one place to run match day, in its own identity, without asking a volunteer to learn software. Every candidate feature was checked against these six questions before it earned a place.

Who

Volunteer coaches, players, and parents at amateur and youth clubs.

What

One white-label app for chat, fixtures, lineups, RSVP, and the table.

Why

The current ten-tool stack is fragile, generic, and re-built every season.

When

Across a match week: midweek planning through Sunday kickoff.

Where

On phones, often pitch-side with one hand and bad signal.

How

Role-based views, a multi-team data model, and an overlay for one-tap actions.

Users

One app, four very different jobs

A coach, a player, a parent, and a club admin open the same app and need almost opposite things.

One shell, role-gated regions
Shipped structureFour roles, one app
Shared shell: the club's identity, the same for everyone who opens it
Role-gated region: a coach gets lineup and schedule controls where a player gets RSVP and the chat
Parent channel, kept separate from the player group rather than a permission on top of it
Role is resolved in the structure rather than by hiding buttons. The parent space is a distinct channel because for under-age teams "same room, fewer rights" was not good enough.

Structural breakdown of how the shipped app is divided by role, not a screenshot and not an original wireframe.

Coach

Builds the lineup, sets the schedule, posts announcements, runs match day from one screen.

Player

Sees fixtures, RSVPs in one tap, reads the team chat, votes for man of the match.

Parent

A dedicated channel, kept separate from the player group, for under-age teams and logistics.

Club admin

Manages multiple teams, roles, and membership across the whole club from one account.

Flows & architecture

From empty club to first whistle

The make-or-break flow is the first ten minutes. Setup leans on what a club already has: phone numbers. A coach creates the club, invites by number, and players are auto-matched into the right team.

Create club→ Invite by phone→ Auto-match players→ Set the lineup→ Run match day

Underneath, I modelled the data around clubs, not single teams: multiple teams per club, role-based access, a parent channel, and membership dues that track manual payments or a provider. Invisible when it works, impossible to retrofit when it does not.

Design Decisions

The club's identity, not mine

Three decisions shaped the product. Team chat also translates messages, so a mixed-language squad can read the group.

Does the app carry its own identity, or the club's?
Chose
White-label by default: club colours, crest, and kit

Belonging was the deepest research insight. People show up for the crest, so the in-app surfaces are built to feel like the club's own product.

Rejected
A generic blue product with Anstoss branding on top

Easier to market and to screenshot, but it makes the app feel like someone else's tool sitting inside the club.

How far should the AI go once it can see the squad data?
Chose
Surface insights as suggestions a coach accepts or ignores

The bench decision stays with the person who has to answer for it on Sunday.

Rejected
AI that picks the lineup

Cost of the choice I made: the most impressive-sounding version of the feature is the one I did not build.

Broad feature set, or a deliberately narrow one?
Chose
Restraint: live scores, lineups, chat, schedule and RSVP, squad, man-of-the-match

Every extra feature is one more thing a volunteer has to ignore. Nothing listed is a roadmap promise, it is what the app does today.

Rejected
Adding features as they tempted their way in

Each candidate was checked against the six define questions first. Cost of the choice I made: the app loses to a broader competitor on a feature-count comparison.

Match day and team chat, rendered in each club's own colours.

Build

I didn't hand this off. I shipped it.

Writing the code myself changed the design: features that only worked in a mockup got cut before they wasted a sprint.

The floating overlay

The signature feature surfaces key actions on top of other apps. Designing it meant living inside real Android constraints, overlay permissions, draw-over behaviour, and battery. It had to be useful in one tap and invisible the rest of the time.

Shipping reality

Getting to the store meant app signing, deep-link verification, and a review queue, the unglamorous work that decides whether a design ever reaches a phone. Coding it with AI build tools kept that loop short enough to learn from.

Testing

Tested against a real season

Because I had built it, I could put it straight into a real club's hands and watch. I dogfooded Anstoss across a run of actual fixtures, tracking where onboarding stalled and whether the overlay helped or got in the way.

The floating overlay, before and after
Early versionsSurfaced too much
A panel of actions sitting on top of whatever app was open
It read fine on a desk. During an actual match it crowded the screen, which is the one place the overlay is supposed to stay out of the way.
RevisedUseful in one tap, invisible otherwise
Only the actions a coach reaches for mid-game
Everything else demoted back into the app itself
Result: the overlay stopped competing with the screen it floats over. Dogfooding across real fixtures, not a review session, is what exposed it.

What I would change: per-club theming is why clubs adopt it, but every club starts from a blank crest and there is no single screenshot of "Anstoss" to show. Next time I would design a strong default identity a club edits down rather than builds up.

Results

Live, and in clubs' own colours

Anstoss is shipped on Android and iOS, free for amateur clubs, with a white-label build per club and the floating overlay and AI features in production. It replaced the ten-tool stack with one app.
