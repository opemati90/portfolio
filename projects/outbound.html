Problem & Context

Trip planning is fragmented, and most people quit it

Planning a trip means juggling a dozen browser tabs, a notes app, a spreadsheet, and three booking sites that do not talk to each other. The hardest moment is the blank page: deciding where to go and what a day should actually look like. Travelers stall there, save a few links, and never finish. The plan ends up half-built in screenshots and group chats, and the trip either gets booked last-minute or not at all.

Outbound starts from a different premise. AI can remove the blank page by drafting a full, day-by-day itinerary in seconds, so the user edits and reacts instead of building from zero. The design problem was not "add AI" but deciding where the AI leads and where the person stays in control, then keeping a live product simple enough that planning, booking, and the trip itself live in one place.

Who

Travelers aged 22-38 who plan on their phone and want a trip shaped around their taste, not a generic list

What

An AI app that drafts a full itinerary from one prompt, then manages bookings and tracks trips you have taken

When

Across the whole arc: inspiration, planning, booking, and the trip itself, mostly in short on-the-go sessions

Where

Mobile-first, live on iOS and the web, designed for thumb reach and one-handed use

Why

Planning is where most trips stall. Killing the blank page is the highest-leverage thing a travel app can do

How

Fast AI draft you can edit and lock, unified booking management, and a passport that rewards finishing trips

The blank page is the real blocker

Interviewees did not struggle to book once they knew the plan. They struggled to commit to a shape for the trip. Seven of nine had a half-finished plan sitting in notes or saved links they never returned to.

Planning lives in scattered tools

A typical trip touched five to nine surfaces: search, maps, a notes app, a spreadsheet, and separate booking sites. Nothing held the whole picture, so the plan existed only in someone's head.

Existing tools ask before they give

The competitive look showed planners that gate value behind sign-up and long preference quizzes. People wanted to see a credible plan first and judge whether the tool understood them before investing anything.

Finishing a trip has no payoff

Once a trip ended it vanished. Nobody had a place that remembered where they had been, which removed any reason to come back between trips and any sense of momentum.

Insight

The job is to remove the first decision, not add features

If the app produces a credible plan the user can react to within the first session, everything downstream gets easier. That reframed the brief from a planning toolbox to a fast draft you stay in control of.

Final Designs

AI-powered, warm, adventure-ready

The orange-and-white palette evokes warmth and adventure. AI generates complete itineraries from natural language prompts. The passport feature tracks trips and collects continent stamps, creating engagement beyond trip planning.

Design Decisions

Four decisions, and what each one cost

Every decision below traded something away. I tried to be explicit about the tradeoff rather than pretend the easy path was free.

Should the first screen be a one-tap magic draft, or a draft the person can take apart?
Chose
A full draft in one pass, with every block editable and lockable

The app generates a day-by-day itinerary immediately, so the first thing you see is a real plan rather than an empty form. Lock the parts you like, regenerate the rest.

Rejected
A pure one-tap magic moment you cannot change

A draft you cannot edit is worse than no draft. Cost of the choice I made: a slower, more deliberate first screen, traded for trust and a sense of authorship.

Does the user sign up before or after they see a plan?
Chose
Generate the first itinerary with no account

Research showed people quit when a tool asks before it gives. Someone who has already seen a plan they like has a real reason to sign up.

Rejected
The sign-up wall and preference quiz the tools I walked all used

Cost of the choice I made: I lose early account capture and some analytics on those anonymous sessions.

Should the reward system pull people back, or push them?
Chose
Pull only, a continent stamped when a trip is done

Finished trips become a collection worth returning to, and the reward stays tied to real travel.

Rejected
Streaks, guilt notifications, daily login bait

Cost of the choice I made: weaker short-term engagement hooks, in exchange for not manufacturing fake urgency.

Unified booking, not another tab

Bookings attach to the itinerary they belong to, so the plan and the reservations stay in one place instead of scattering across email and apps. Tradeoff: deeper integration work and more states to design, but it is the only way the app holds the whole trip rather than being one more silo.

Validation

The passport screen was the emotional peak

Finding: nearly every tester reacted out loud at the Passport screen, the only point in the flow where that happened. Decision: I treated it as the app's signature moment rather than a side feature, pulling the continent stamp into onboarding so people meet it before they have even taken a trip. Result: the moment now anchors the product's identity, and "Passport Approved" became the single most-commented-on detail in later sessions.

Where the passport is first met, before and after
First buildPassport met at the end of the flow
Onboard→ Generate a trip→ Take the trip→ First continent stamp, discovered late or not at all
RevisedThe signature moment, moved to the front
Onboard→ Continent stamp, before any trip exists→ Generate a trip→ Take the trip

The stamp was the only point in the flow testers reacted to out loud, and it sat at the end of the flow, behind a discovery task testers had to complete first. Pulling it into onboarding means people meet it before they have taken a trip. Passport discovery scored 88% in testing. Only the revised order shipped.

Critical

Auth errors lost people at the worst moment

Finding: a generic "Invalid code" on the OTP step left testers stuck and unsure whether to wait, retry, or restart. Decision: I rewrote the copy to say what to do next, "Code does not match, check your email," and added a visible resend timer so the path forward was never ambiguous. Result: testers in the follow-up round recovered from a bad code without dropping out, turning a dead end into a self-service fix.

The OTP dead end, before and after

RevisedTesters recovered without dropping out

Same two screens, different exit. The change was copy plus one visible timer, so the path forward was never ambiguous. Only the revised version shipped.

Reflections

AI should draft, not decide

The strongest version of the product was not the most automated one. People trusted the AI more when they could overrule it. The control I gave back through editable, lockable blocks was what made the speed feel safe instead of pushy.

Emotional milestones in onboarding

The "Passport Approved" moment was the single most commented-on decision. Onboarding should not just be functional. It should make users feel something.

Error states are conversations

"Oops!" is not helpful. "The code does not match, we just sent a new one" is. Every error state is a conversation with an anxious user.

Shipping changes the questions

Testing told me what confused people; living traffic tells me what they ignore. The next round is less about polish and more about earning the second session.
