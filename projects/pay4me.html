Framing the Problem

5W1H: Defining the design challenge

Paying foreign tuition is a high-stakes, cross-border, trust-sensitive moment. For most families it is the largest single transfer they have ever made, often the first time they have moved money across borders at all. The existing marketing site asked people to start that transfer before it had earned their confidence, and the onboarding form treated identity verification as paperwork rather than reassurance.

Who

International students sending tuition and the parents who sponsor them, mostly first-time cross-border senders.

What

A trust-first marketing site and onboarding flow for high-value education transfers.

When

During admission and enrollment windows, under hard university payment deadlines.

Where

Web. People research on mobile, then complete the payment on desktop alongside their bank.

Why

Most senders had never paid tuition abroad before. The old site led with product features, not proof, and the funnel leaked hardest at sign-up and verification.

How

Lead with trust signals and named outcomes, then make fees, FX, and live payment status visible at every step.

Research and Discovery

Where confidence broke down

I ran interviews with international students and sponsoring parents, then walked the existing funnel analytics step by step to see where intent turned into abandonment. The pattern was consistent: people did not drop out because the product was hard to use. They dropped out because at each step they were asked to trust the platform with more, and the interface gave them less to trust.

Funnel drop-off

The break was at sign-up and KYC, not pricing

Most abandonment clustered at account creation and identity verification, not at the fee disclosure. People reached the verification step willing to pay, then stalled when asked to upload documents with no explanation of why or what happened next.

Trust barrier

"How do I know this is not a scam?"

First-time senders looked for the same reassurance signals their bank gives them: a real company, named universities, and confirmation that the money actually landed. The old site led with marketing copy instead, which read as a warning rather than a welcome.

KYC friction

Verification felt like an interrogation

The onboarding asked for documents up front with no framing. Parents in particular wanted to know which document, why it was needed, and that their data was handled responsibly before they would proceed.

Channel split

Research on mobile, pay on desktop

People compared options on their phone, then switched to a laptop to complete the transfer next to their online banking. A flow that only felt finished on one device lost the people who moved between them.

Design Decisions

Key decisions, and the tradeoffs behind them

Proof first, or product first?
Chose
Trust before product

The site opens with concrete proof: named universities supported, country coverage, and real sender outcomes, before any feature copy. For a first-time high-stakes transfer, earning belief outranks shaving a scroll.

Alternative
Feature-led hero, as the old site had it

Cost of the call: the hero is slower to reach the call to action. The old feature-led version read as a warning rather than a welcome to first-time senders.

Show the full cost before sign-up, or at the end?
Chose
Transparent FX and fees up front

The full cost, including the exchange rate and any fee, is shown before sign-up. Hidden costs were the single fastest way to lose a sender's trust.

Alternative
Disclose at the end of the flow

Cost of the call: showing the margin early can look less competitive at a glance.

How much of the transfer's progress does the business expose?
Chose
Live status from initiated to received

Every transfer shows where the money is, with timestamps, through to receipt by the university. Visibility was exactly what parents asked for, and it cut payment-status support tickets 27% in design-stage testing.

Alternative
Confirmation only, no intermediate states

Cost of the call: live status commits the business to surfacing delays it would rather not advertise.

One dense verification form, or several explained steps?
Chose
Simplified, explained KYC

Verification breaks into short steps, each stating which document is needed, why, and how the data is handled. The framing turned the most abandoned step into one people were willing to finish.

Alternative
A single dense document upload

Cost of the call: a few extra screens. That was the version people described as an interrogation.

People were being asked to hand over documents before they knew what the transfer would cost. Two steps swapped, plus a reason attached to each request. Completion through verification rose noticeably in the next round, and onboarding completion finished 31% up, with the largest gain at this step.

Reflections

What I learned

Trust is sequenced, not stated

You cannot claim trustworthiness in a headline. People grant it step by step, so each screen has to give a reason to take the next one. Reordering when we asked for verification mattered more than how the form itself looked.

Transparency is cheaper than support

Showing fees, FX, and live status up front felt risky to the business, but it removed the questions that drove the most anxiety and the most support tickets. Honesty earlier paid for itself later.

What I would do differently

I tested with 8 to 12 people, enough to find the big trust breaks but too few to trust the percentages. I also leaned on the platform's own scale numbers in the hero before separating them from my measured results, which blurs what the redesign actually moved. Next time I would size the sample for the claims I want to make and label borrowed metrics from the first draft.
